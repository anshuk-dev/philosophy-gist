Indian Philosophy • UNIT_01 ID: IND_META_001

Satkāryavāda vs. Asatkāryavāda (Pariṇāmavāda vs. Ārambhavāda)

School: Sāṃkhya vs. Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika | Topic: Metaphysics: Theories of Causation
1. The Core Dilemma (The Problem)
"Does the effect (kārya) already pre-exist potentially in the cause (kāraṇa) prior to its empirical manifestation, or is the effect an entirely new entity brought into existence de novo?"
2. Technical Lexicon & Philosophical Vocabulary
Satkāryavāda (सत्कार्यवाद) — The doctrine that the effect pre-exists in its material cause in an unmanifest (avyakta) state.
Pariṇāmavāda (परिणामवाद) — Real transformation; the cause actually transforms into the effect (held by Sāṃkhya and Viśiṣṭādvaita).
Asatkāryavāda / Ārambhavāda (असत्कार्यवाद / आरम्भवाद) — The doctrine of fresh origination: the effect is non-existent before production; causal operation produces a genuinely new substance.
Vivartavāda (विवर्तवाद) — Apparent or illusory transformation; the cause appears as the effect without undergoing real ontological change (Advaita Vedānta).
3. The Dialectic: Purvapaksha vs. Siddhanta
Pūrvapakṣa (The Opponent's Challenge)
The Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika argues: If the pot already existed inside the clay lump, the potter's effort (kārakavyāpāra) would be utterly redundant. Moreover, we do not fetch water with a lump of clay; the pot serves a practical function (arthakriyā) that clay cannot. Therefore, the pot is a completely new entity produced at a specific moment.
Siddhānta (Philosophical Defense & Resolution)
Īśvarakṛṣṇa in the Sāṃkhyakārikā provides five classic proofs for Satkāryavāda: (1) Asad-akaraṇāt (what does not exist cannot be produced, e.g., oil from sand); (2) Upādāna-grahaṇāt (specific materials must be chosen for specific effects); (3) Sarvasambhavābhāvāt (not everything can produce anything); (4) Śaktasya śakya-karaṇāt (a competent cause produces only what it has the potential for); (5) Kāraṇa-bhāvāc-ca (the effect is of the identical essence as the cause). The potter merely manifests (abhivyakti) what was latent.
4. Primary Canonical Source Citation
Author: Īśvarakṛṣṇa • Work: Sāṃkhyakārikā • Locus: Kārikā 9 ('asadakaraṇād upādānagrahaṇāt... satkāryam').
5. Selection Committee / University Interview Pitfall
⚠ Interview Trap Question
Carefully separate the two forms of Satkāryavāda: Sāṃkhya's 'Pariṇāma-vāda' (real transformation, Prakṛti actually evolves into Mahat, Ahaṃkāra, etc.) versus Śaṅkara's 'Vivarta-vāda' (the world is an illusory superimposition on unchanging Brahman). Both accept Satkāryavāda, but differ radically on the ontological status of the modification.
6. Wisdom Gist (Reflective Synthesis)
"Nothing emerges out of sheer ontological nothingness. Transformation is the unfolding of inherent latency, reminding us that potentiality precedes actuality in all creative and intellectual life."
Western Philosophy • UNIT_03 ID: WEST_META_001

Hume's Problem of Induction & Critique of Necessary Connection

School: British Empiricism | Topic: Epistemology: Causation & Inductive Skepticism
1. The Core Dilemma (The Problem)
"Can we logically justify our universal expectation that unobserved instances will resemble observed instances, or that causes contain a necessary power to produce their effects?"
2. Technical Lexicon & Philosophical Vocabulary
Constant Conjunction — The observable empirical relation where event A is repeatedly followed by event B in time and space, without any observable nexus of power.
Necessary Connection — The metaphysical assumption of an intrinsic, unbreakable tie between cause and effect, which Hume declares empirically undetectable.
Custom or Habit (Sittliche Gewohnheit) — The psychological disposition of the human animal that projects necessity onto nature based on repeated past associations.
Uniformity of Nature — The inductive premise that the future will resemble the past, which cannot be justified demonstratively or inductively without begging the question (petitio principii).
3. The Dialectic: Purvapaksha vs. Siddhanta
Pūrvapakṣa (The Opponent's Challenge)
Aristotelian-Scholastic and Cartesian rationalists asserted that cause and effect possess an intelligible necessary connection discoverable by reason (e.g., an effect cannot contain more perfection or reality than its cause).
Siddhānta (Philosophical Defense & Resolution)
Hume shows in the Enquiry and Treatise that if we inspect any instance of causation (e.g., billiard ball A striking ball B), we observe contiguity in space and priority in time, but NEVER the supposed 'power' or 'necessary connection'. If we seek to justify induction by appealing to past regularities, we presuppose the very principle of induction we sought to prove, falling into circular reasoning. Induction is grounded in animal habit, not rational proof.
4. Primary Canonical Source Citation
Author: David Hume • Work: An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding • Locus: Section IV ('Sceptical Doubts concerning the Operations of the Understanding') and Section VII ('Of the Idea of Necessary Connection').
5. Selection Committee / University Interview Pitfall
⚠ Interview Trap Question
Selection committees often ask: 'Did Hume deny causation?' The trap is to say yes. Hume never denied that events occur in regular succession or that humans must act on causal expectations; he denied that reason can demonstrate the necessary connection or provide a non-circular epistemic foundation for induction.
6. Wisdom Gist (Reflective Synthesis)
"Humility is the pinnacle of philosophy. Much of what human beings dogmatically label 'absolute universal law' is an instinctive projection of animal habit onto a universe that never promised us certainty."

Dialectical Bridge: Comparative Insight

While Sāṃkhya resolves causation through ontological latency (Satkāryavāda—the effect is the cause unfolded), Hume disintegrates the causal nexus altogether into psychological association. For Sāṃkhya, cause and effect share identical essence (kāraṇa-bhāva); for Hume, cause and effect are distinct events with zero necessary connection discoverable by reason.

Central University Interview & UGC NET Practice MCQs

Q1. Which argument for Satkāryavāda in Sāṃkhyakārikā asserts that 'oil cannot be pressed from sand'?
  • (A) Upādānagrahaṇāt
  • (B) Asad-akaraṇāt
  • (C) Śaktasya śakya-karaṇāt
  • (D) Kāraṇa-bhāvāt
Correct: (B) — 'Asad-akaraṇāt' (what does not exist cannot be brought into being) establishes that non-existent entities cannot be produced; if the effect were non-existent, even sand would yield oil.
Q2. Hume locates the origin of our belief in causal necessity in:
  • (A) A priori rational demonstration
  • (B) Direct intellectual intuition of power
  • (C) Custom or psychological habit of mind
  • (D) Innate Cartesian ideas
Correct: (C) — Hume concludes that after experiencing constant conjunction, custom or habit alone induces the mind to expect the attendant effect.
